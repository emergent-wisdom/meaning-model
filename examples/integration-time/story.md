# Integration Time

The docking tube was forty metres of ribbed white fabric, and the Sun was in it.

It came through a port halfway along, a point so bright that Graciela put up her hand without thinking, and in the unlit tube her hand threw a shadow on the wall with edges as clean as a cut. She had read that it would be like this: at six hundred and seventy-two astronomical units the Sun was three seconds of arc across, which is to say a point, and about as bright as a full Moon over Valparaíso. Reading it had not prepared her for the shadow. Nothing in eleven months on the *Starling* had cast one.

Behind her Kaya, who with Brandt made up the *Starling*'s entire crew, said, "Sixteen days," the way he had said it every morning of the approach, as if it were a greeting. The *Starling* was a fusion torch, and it had averaged one point two per cent of the speed of light to get here; it carried food and propellant for the voyage out, sixteen days docked and the voyage home, and nothing for a seventeenth day. On the sixteenth day the ship would leave, with the autonomy installed or not, and with the station's resident aboard.

"I know," she said, and pulled herself on.

Through the next port there was nothing to see, which was correct. Twelve one-metre telescopes held formation out there across a patch of dark three kilometres wide, the image plane, where the Sun's gravity brought one planet's light to a focus. They flew with their lights off, so as not to see themselves.

The hatch at the end said FOCAL STATION T1E in agency letters, and under that, on a strip of tape, in a hand she knew better than her own: *the Landing*. It opened before she touched it, onto a ladder down the spoke. The Landing turned slowly about the line to the Sun, and halfway down the ladder she had weight again.

Her mother was smaller than her handwriting. That was the first thing, and it was unfair, and Graciela noticed herself noticing it. Candelaria Araya had been cutting her own hair since 2220, and it stood up grey on one side where the scissors had not reached. She was holding a mug in both hands. She did not put it down.

Neither of them spoke for long enough that it became a decision. Graciela could feel the old habit arriving: the message composed, the message sent, ninety-three hours out and ninety-three back, and between them the long flat time in which nothing was required of you. There was no such time here. Her mother was a metre away, breathing.

"Did you sleep?" her mother said. "On the way. Kaya said the bunks are bad." And then, before Graciela could decide anything: "Do you want tea? I have tea. Real leaves."

"Eleven months, mamá," Graciela said. "I slept."

Her mother nodded as if that answered the tea as well, and it was only later, sitting in the galley with a mug she had not asked for, that Graciela understood she had been given the tea anyway.

The galley was one person wide. There was one chair, bolted at the counter, and beside it a supply crate with a cushion tied on with cord, and she understood that the crate had been a chair for no one for eleven years and was being a chair for her now. The tea was in a jar labelled in her mother's hand, *2219*. Her mother kept the galley dim, and the port had its blind down; through the hole where the blind's cord ran, sunlight lay on the counter in a spot no bigger than a lentil. It was the whole Sun.

"Chela," her mother said, and stopped. "Graciela."

"Either."

"You sign them G. Mardones."

"That's my name."

"It's half your name. The other half is mine." Her mother turned the mug. "I called you Chela until I left. After that I thought I should— you were eighteen."

For eleven years her mother had written to her nearly every day, long careful messages that read like letters, an hour of composing at a time. Graciela had answered, when she answered, days later and in four lines. The messages had begun *Chela* until the year her mother left and *Graciela* ever after, and Graciela had never asked why, because a question took ninety-three hours to arrive and ninety-three to come back, and by then she would have had to mean it.

"You wrote me two days before you launched," her mother said.

"I know."

"I answered."

"I read it on the ship." Eleven days out. It had said *don't*, and then a great deal more, and it had arrived, as she had arranged, when *don't* was a word about the past.

"You timed it."

"You told me about the extensions after you'd signed them," Graciela said. "Both times. I got the first one in the middle of my exams."

Her mother looked at the lentil of sunlight on the counter. "The agency needed an answer."

"So did I."

It was not how she had rehearsed it. In the rehearsals there had been a great deal more of the eleven years, said in order. Instead she said, "I'd like to start tonight," and her mother said, "They're really closing me," and it was not clear which of them was answering whom.

"The order's from 2229," Graciela said. "I'm what they sent."

"I know what they sent."

The console was in the next module, with a second chair that was also a crate. Graciela laid her kit out on the deck in the order she would need it, which was the order she had packed it in, which was the only order she trusted. First the clock: she synchronized the station's to the *Starling*'s, found them four tenths of a second apart, logged the difference, and felt better. Then the pipeline.

"Single-window difference imaging," she said, because her mother was standing behind her and not asking. "Each time La Mancha comes round, it compares that one window with your static model and flags whatever changed. One window is far too noisy to make a map from. It isn't too noisy to disagree with one. Mostly it's a fault detector: something drifts, it tells you before you'd see it."

"Before I'd see it," her mother said.

"Before anyone would."

"Is there someone?" her mother asked a while later, while the first calibration ran. "At home."

Graciela watched the progress bar. She thought about the question for the length of the calibration, which was four minutes, which was nothing, which was the fastest she had answered her mother in a decade.

"Tomás," she said. "He asked me not to come. When I came anyway he said he'd wait. Two years."

Her mother said nothing to that, and Graciela found she was grateful, and that she could not have said so in a letter either.

On the main screen the coronagraph held the Sun behind a black disc, and around the disc, a hair's breadth out, lay a ring of faint red light. "It's never a picture," her mother said. "Every point on that ring has the whole planet in it." The light in the ring had left TRAPPIST-1e forty years ago, ten years before Graciela was born.

Her mother leaned past her, which put her arm against Graciela's shoulder for the first time since she was eighteen, and brought up the map. The planet kept one face to its red star. On the eastern edge of the day side, where it began to go dark, lay a region the colour of a bruise.

"La Mancha," Graciela said, before her mother could. The stain, and the plain in Spain with the wheat and the windmills; she had been twenty when the joke arrived in a letter, and twenty-six when the letter came that said it was alive. Nine hundred kilometres of something photosynthetic, black across everything a red star gave it.

"Eleven years of light, at three point eight kilometres a pixel," her mother said. "It hasn't changed by one."

Graciela looked at the dark, even texture of it. It looked, she thought, like a very long exposure of nothing happening.

"It's the steadiest thing I know," her mother said.

* * *

On the eighth day Candelaria woke at three, as she had woken at three for as long as she had lived here, and found the console light already on.

Her daughter was asleep on the crate with her back against the module frame and a data cable looped twice around her wrist, the way you tie up a boat. For a week she had slept like that, in pieces, between calibrations. She ate standing. She said "copy" instead of "yes". Candelaria had catalogued these things the way she catalogued anything that arrived at the station, carefully and without conclusions, and she had not yet found the question they answered.

At 03:40 La Mancha came round into the window, and the pipeline woke, and so did Graciela, all at once, the cable already in her other hand.

The first live difference map took eleven minutes. Most of it was nothing: a grey field of noise, which is what an unchanged world looks like when you subtract it from itself. Across La Mancha there were stripes.

They were faint and parallel and evenly spaced, a dozen of them, and the fault detector drew a box around them and wrote CALIBRATION FAULT — ALIGNMENT, T07? in the yellow the agency kept for things you could fix.

"Mierda," Graciela said quietly. "That's mine." She was already pulling telescope seven's alignment logs. "Seven's been drifting since the approach. I thought I'd trimmed it."

Candelaria looked at the stripes. She had looked at La Mancha for a decade and it had never had stripes, and she felt, for a moment, the particular embarrassment of an old instrument caught out by a new one.

"They stop at the coast," she said.

Graciela did not look up. "What?"

"They stop where the mat stops. An alignment fault would stripe the whole frame."

Graciela looked. She was quiet for a while, the logs still scrolling under her hand.

"Run it on the last window," Candelaria said.

"The pipeline wasn't calibrated a week ago."

"The raw data is there. Every window since '20 is there." She heard her voice come out in the tone she kept for the agency, and did not stop it. "If we ask Earth, the answer comes back after the next window has gone. One orbit is six days; a question and its answer take nearly eight. Nobody can steer this from there."

Her daughter looked at her then, properly, for perhaps the first time that week. Then she turned back to the console and began to set up the run.

It took most of the night. The pipeline reprojected both windows, the one from a week ago and the one from tonight, out of the instrument's frame and onto the ground, onto the planet's own latitude and longitude, as if the twelve telescopes had never existed. Graciela fitted the edges of the stripes against the static model to a fraction of a pixel, because that was how she did everything.

At 06:12 the two maps lay over each other on the screen. The coast of La Mancha sat exactly on itself, so the overlay was true, and the stripes sat on the ground with it. Not on the detector. On the ground. Only their bright edges had moved, each one east by the same 3.1 kilometres, in one orbit.

"An artifact lives in the instrument's frame," Candelaria said. Her mouth was dry. "This lives in theirs."

"Whose?" Graciela said.

Neither of them answered.

Graciela put both hands flat on the console, as if it might move, and laughed once, a short surprised sound Candelaria had last heard from her at eight, when a kite went up on the first try.

Then the arithmetic came, because it always came. A dozen swaths, eight kilometres wide and seventy-five apart, were moving east together, so any one place was cut again every five months, just as it had grown back; in the years Candelaria had been watching, every part of La Mancha had been cut twenty-seven times. Her change checks had been built for clouds: they compared each orbit with the last at forty kilometres a pixel, where a swath that has moved on three kilometres, cut at one edge and regrowing behind, changes a pixel less than a thin cloud would.

She had integrated it away. The patience she was best at had kept for her, faithfully, the average of all that cutting: the even dark on her map. The field had never once held still.

She found that she was standing in the galley with the kettle in her hand, and did not remember going there. She thought about the ones who cut. She thought about light that had left forty years ago, about whatever had done this before her daughter was born, which might have stopped since or might be doing it tonight, in a present she would never see. She thought about 2220, her first month here, and how she had decided then that the field was quiet. She did not, she noticed later, think about her daughter at all.

She wrote the report the next day, the way she wrote everything that went to Earth: slowly, and more than once. First she tried to find a way for no one to be doing it. On Earth's dry slopes, tiger bush arranged itself into bands like these with nobody cutting anything, but those bands bent with the ground and forked, and no two kept quite the same pace. She went through all twelve. They ran straight, none of them forked, and every one had moved the same 3.1 kilometres, to a tenth of a pixel. The fir waves were harder. On windy mountainsides whole bands of fir died on the side facing the wind while seedlings came up behind them, so the bands walked downwind, straight and in step; nothing on TRAPPIST-1e was steadier than its wind, and nothing said how fast such a wave would walk under a red sun. That one she could not rule out from here. *Interpretation: consistent with cultivation by an agent. A grazing front or a wind-driven die-back wave is not excluded.* She took out *consistent with* and put it back. She took out *by an agent* and put it back. She thought of a committee on Earth reading it in four days, and wrote it plainer.

At 14:20 on the tenth day she sent it, and it began its ninety-three hours. Any answer would reach the station on the eighteenth day. The *Starling* would leave on the sixteenth.

When she looked up the console module was empty. The crate had its cushion and its cord and no one on it. On the comms panel the *Starling*'s crew channel showed a message being composed, outbound, to Earth, a long one; the counter said it had been open for an hour and twelve minutes.

She had not heard her daughter leave.

* * *

The window on the fourteenth came in at 06:05, and the edges had moved again: 3.1 kilometres, the same as before, as if whoever was doing it kept a calendar. Three windows now, one orbit apart, agreeing. Graciela ran the reprojection twice and logged it and did not tell her mother that she had run it twice.

On the fifteenth she crossed to the *Starling* at six in the morning, before her mother was up, and found Kaya in the cockpit doing the departure checklist with a bulb of coffee held in his teeth.

"I'm not coming," she said. "I wrote to the agency on the tenth; they've had it since yesterday. I'll sign whatever they need. The station needs someone in the loop; you've heard her, a question to Earth takes longer than an orbit. My mother's done eleven years. She goes home. I stay."

Kaya took the coffee out of his mouth and looked at her for a long moment.

"What."

"Your mother," Kaya said, "was here an hour ago. She's staying, and you're going home to your Tomás, and she'd take it as a kindness if I didn't let you argue."

Graciela stood in the cockpit door with her hand on the frame. She thought, with great clarity, of every message she had ever timed. She thought of her mother at five in the morning in this same doorway, choosing her words the way she chose them for the agency, and doing it first, so that by the time Graciela knew, it would already be arranged.

"She timed it," she said.

Kaya put the coffee back in his teeth. "I leave at noon tomorrow. You two work out which of you I'm taking. I'd like to know by dinner."

Her mother was in the galley, in the one chair, with two mugs on the counter. She had not drunk from either.

"Kaya told you," she said.

"Kaya told me."

"Chela—" Her mother stopped, and this time did not correct it. "Tomás said two years. You told me that the first night. Two years is— he's waiting now, today. That doesn't keep." She turned one of the mugs a quarter turn. "You go home. I'll write. I'll write every day, I've had the practice."

It was the most she had said at once since the arrival, and none of it was in order, and Graciela understood that her mother had not rehearsed it, or had rehearsed it and lost the order on the way.

"You'll write," Graciela said. And then, because there were no ninety-three hours to hide in, and no one to arrange it for her, she said the rest.

"You wrote to me every day, mamá. I have all of them. I used to read them at night and I could hear you choosing the words. And I answered in four lines, four days later. I'm not going home to wait for your letters. I got so good at waiting that I made Tomás do it."

Her mother said nothing for a long time. Then she said, "The stores are fourteen hundred and sixty person-days."

"I know what the stores are."

"Two of us is two years. The next ship is four."

"Kaya can leave what he brought for us going home," Graciela said. "Six hundred and seventy."

"Nearly three years, then." Her mother did the division without looking up, and then the other one. "Or four years at seventy-three per cent. We'd be hungry the whole time. It's a bet. That they read the report and send a ship early. That they believe it."

"They'll believe it."

"We can't know that before noon tomorrow."

"No," Graciela said. "We can't know anything before noon tomorrow. That's what it's like here. You told me."

Graciela thought of her mother alone here for four more years, sixty-two, with the only thing like it anyone had ever seen and no one to tell in less than four days.

"A wind wave would do everything I've shown them," her mother said. "Showing them it isn't one is work for two."

Graciela waited.

"And I wanted to stay," her mother said. "Before the stripes. I'd have found a reason."

"I know."

"Both, then," her mother said.

"Both."

She wrote to Tomás in the console module, on the crate. It took an hour and it was not four lines. It ended with the date of the next ship and one sentence she rewrote six times: *I am asking you to wait, which is the thing I never forgave anyone for asking.* When it was done she set the send time for 12:05 tomorrow, five minutes after the *Starling* would be gone, and looked at that for a while.

"Send it now," her mother said from the door.

"He can't answer in time. It gets there after the ship's gone either way."

"That isn't what it's for." Her mother did not come in. "Send it now, and it leaves while you could still change your mind. He should have that."

Graciela thought about a message that had arrived when *don't* was a word about the past. She cleared the send time, and sent it, and it began its ninety-three hours to Valparaíso.

At noon on the sixteenth Kaya and Brandt took the *Starling* off the collar. They watched from the galley port. For a few minutes it was a shape, its drive cold until it was well clear of them, and then it was a point, and then it was a point that moved, which was the only way to tell it from a star.

When it was gone Graciela did the division again, although she knew the answer. Seventy-three per cent, for four years, unless someone believed them.

Afterwards her mother took the one chair and Graciela took the crate, and they began on the next window, which was four days out. "If we put seven and nine in the near infrared," her mother said, "a dying edge should come up as a slope, and a cut one—"

"—as a step, by the second pass," Graciela said. "I've already queued it."

Her mother looked at her. On the counter between them the Sun lay in its spot the size of a lentil, and neither of them said anything about how long the answer had taken.
