# The Book of Conditions: era package

This is the research the book starts from: what was true in Britain up to August 1843 about Charles Babbage's
calculating engines, the people around them and the world they worked in. It is real history only. Nothing in it is

## The engines

**The Difference Engine.** Babbage designed a machine to calculate and print mathematical tables by the method of
finite differences. Tables of the day were computed by hand and copied, and their errors were a known problem: for
navigation, for astronomy, for insurance. In 1823 the government gave him £1,700 to begin. The engineer Joseph Clement
built it in his workshop from 1824. In 1832 a small working part was assembled, about one seventh of the whole, which
worked with six-digit numbers and second differences. Work on the full engine stopped in 1833, in a dispute with
Clement over the high cost of his precision tools and over who owned them; by the custom of the trade, a workman kept
the tools he made. By 1842 more than £17,000 of public money had been spent. That year Robert Peel's government, on
the advice of the Astronomer Royal, George Biddell Airy, ended its support. Babbage met Peel to reverse the decision;
the meeting went badly.

**The Analytical Engine.** After the Difference Engine stalled, Babbage designed a far more general machine: a store
holding numbers and a mill operating on them, directed by punched cards of the kind Jacquard looms used. Between 1837
and 1840 he wrote programs for it. In 1840, at Giovanni Plana's invitation, he explained it in Turin, the only public
account he gave. Luigi Federico Menabrea wrote that account up in French, published in October 1842. Nothing of the
Analytical Engine was built in his lifetime.

**The translation and the Notes.** Ada Lovelace translated Menabrea's paper and added her own Notes, lettered A to G,
far longer than the paper itself. By February 1843 she had settled on this plan, and by the end of July the Notes were
essentially finished. They appeared in 1843 in the third volume of Richard Taylor's *Scientific Memoirs*, London,
signed only "A.A.L.".

**August 1843.** Babbage wanted an unsigned preface of his own published with the translation, explaining how the
government had failed the engines. Lovelace thought it a bad idea and refused. When he suggested withdrawing the
whole publication without it, she was furious. The translation appeared without the preface.

On 14 August 1843 she wrote him a long letter, sixteen pages. She offered to "conduct the business for you", with his
"own undivided energies being devoted to the execution of the work", on conditions she set out. Babbage wrote on the
letter: "Saw A.A.L. this morning and refused all the conditions." Their friendship survived. Within days he wrote
about visiting with drawings, and she told her mother they were "more friends than ever". The collaboration on the
Engine went no further.

## The people

**Charles Babbage**, 26 December 1791 to 18 October 1871. Born into a prosperous banking family, he was educated at
Cambridge, where in 1812 he helped found the Analytical Society. In 1814 he married Georgiana Whitmore, who died in
1827. He helped found the Astronomical Society in 1820, and his *Reflections on the Decline of Science* (1830) helped
bring about the British Association in 1831. He was Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at Cambridge from 1828 to 1839.
He lived in Marylebone, London, and spent much of his life and fortune on mechanical calculation.

**Ada Lovelace**, 10 December 1815 to 27 November 1852. The daughter of Lord Byron and Annabella Milbanke, she was given
an unusually serious mathematical education for a woman of her time, encouraged by Mary Somerville. She met Babbage on
5 June 1833, aged seventeen, at a party at his London house, and soon after saw a demonstration of the Difference
Engine. In 1835 she married William King. From about 1840, Augustus De Morgan of University College London taught her
calculus, mostly by correspondence. Her health was often poor.

**William King**, 21 February 1805 to 29 December 1893. A landowner, he married Ada Byron in 1835 and was created Earl of
Lovelace and Viscount Ockham in 1838. Their children were Byron (1836), Anne (1837) and Ralph (1839). His homes
included Ockham Park in Surrey and, later, Horsley Towers in East Horsley.

**Joseph Clement**, 13 June 1779 to 28 February 1844. Born at Great Asby in Westmorland, he worked as a weaver and slater,
then made looms and learned draughtsmanship. From 1814 he worked for Joseph Bramah, then as chief draughtsman at
Maudslay, Sons and Field in Lambeth. In 1817 he set up his own workshop at 21 Prospect Place, Newington, south of the
Thames. He built Babbage's Difference Engine from 1824 to 1833. In 1828 he urged a standard system of screw threads.

**Joseph Whitworth**, 21 December 1803 to 22 January 1887. Born in Stockport, he trained as a mechanic and worked in
London for Henry Maudslay, Holtzapffel and Joseph Clement, helping to make the Difference Engine. In 1833 he set up his
own machine-tool firm in Manchester. In 1841 he proposed a standard screw thread, with a fixed angle and a set pitch for
each diameter.

**Griffith Davies**, 5 December 1788 to 21 March 1855. Born in Llandwrog, Caernarfonshire, he rose from modest beginnings
through mathematics to become advisory actuary to the Guardian Assurance Company. The company's success was largely
credited to his tables. In 1831 he wrote twenty-four reports on calculating life contingencies, and the same year he was
elected a Fellow of the Royal Society.

**Henry Prevost Babbage**, 1824 to 1918. Charles Babbage's son, he served in the Bengal Army of the East India Company.

**Mary Somerville**, 26 December 1780 to 29 November 1872. Born in Jedburgh, largely self-taught, she became famous with
*Mechanism of the Heavens* (1831), her rendering of Laplace, and *On the Connexion of the Physical Sciences* (1834).
She was a mentor and friend to Ada Lovelace, and with her husband she lived for most of her later life in Italy.

**Augustus De Morgan**, 1806 to 1871. The first professor of mathematics at University College London, and Lovelace's
teacher.

## The world they worked in

**Calculation.** Tables of logarithms, of astronomy and navigation, and of interest and mortality were computed by
people, by hand. Copying, checking and printing them were each a source of error.

**Life assurance.** Offices such as the Guardian Assurance Company sold policies priced from mortality tables. An
actuary's craft was to make a financial promise answer to evidence of how long people live.

**Precision engineering.** London's machine shops, Bramah's, Maudslay's and Clement's, trained the mechanics who built
the machine tools of the age. Precision was costly, and screw threads were not yet standard: Clement urged a standard in
1828, and Whitworth proposed his in 1841.

**Money.** Prices were in pounds, shillings and pence. For scale: the government spent a little over £17,000 on the
Difference Engine between 1823 and 1842.

**Communication.** The Uniform Penny Post began in January 1840, and letters were the ordinary way to work at a
distance. Railways were spreading; the line between London and Birmingham opened in 1838. The electric telegraph was
in its first years.

**Women's access.** Ability did not differ by sex, but access did. Universities, learned societies and most
professions were closed to women. A married woman's property passed to her husband. A woman like Lovelace could do
serious mathematics through her class, private teaching and correspondence. But she could hold no formal office.

## What really happened afterwards, briefly

- Clement died in February 1844.
- Babbage went on designing without building: a second Difference Engine in the late 1840s, and the Analytical
  Engine all his life. He died in 1871.
- Lovelace died of cancer in November 1852, aged thirty-six.
- Davies died in March 1855.
- Whitworth's firm became one of the leading engineering works in Britain. He died in 1887.
- Somerville died in Naples in 1872, and William King in 1893.
- Henry Prevost Babbage later edited his father's papers on the engines (*Babbage's Calculating Engines*, 1889). He
  built a working portion of the Analytical Engine's mill, and he died in 1918.
